## THREAD 1: "The NFL Draft Has a Math Problem"
*Article 1 — The Quasi-Sharpe Ratio*

**1/ The NFL Draft Has a Math Problem**

I built a Sharpe ratio for NFL draft picks — the same risk-adjusted framework Wall Street uses for investments, applied to every position and draft slot since 2010.

The results challenge a lot of conventional draft wisdom. Here's what the data says. 🧵

[Sharpe heatmap chart]

**2/ The Setup**

A draft pick returns value two ways:
- **Rookie surplus**: starter production at a fraction of FA cost for 4 years
- **Second contract**: the market's verdict on whether the player was worth it

The Sharpe ratio combines both, adjusted for risk (variance) and the cost of just signing a free agent instead.

**3/ The Formula**

Sharpe = [P(elite) × Elite Threshold - FA Replacement Cost] / Volatility

Higher = better risk-adjusted return vs. free agency.
Positive = the draft beats the FA market.
Negative = you're better off signing a free agent.

**4/ QB Is King**

QB leads all positions in the top 10. Why?
- 73% of top-10 QBs produce elite returns
- FA QBs cost 14.6% of the cap — massive rookie surplus
- Second-contract ceilings are the highest in football

The math overwhelmingly favors QB early. The caveat: scouting QBs is brutally hard.

**5/ The Late First Round Is a Sweet Spot**

OT and EDGE don't peak in the top 10 — they peak in the late first. The hit rates stay strong, the rookie surplus is still meaningful, and you're paying less draft capital.

If you can trade back and still land an OT or EDGE, the model says do it.

**6/ WR Value Lives in Round 2**

The best WR Sharpe is in Round 2, not Round 1. DK Metcalf (pick 64), Davante Adams (pick 53), A.J. Brown (pick 51) — all returned more than any top-10 RB since 2010.

WR talent is deep enough that Day 2 picks are competitive with the late first.

**7/ RB Is Negative Everywhere**

RB shows negative Sharpe across every tier. The worst position to draft in Round 1. Three reasons:

- FA RBs are cheap (6% of cap — lowest replacement cost)
- Second-contract ceiling is structurally capped
- Even bellcows only play ~56% of snaps (committee splits)

**8/ The Saquon Problem**

Saquon Barkley — pick #2, 2018. He was a hit. He started. He produced.

But his total return is well below the elite threshold. His second contract was a fraction of what a QB or WR earns. Even when the pick *works*, the return doesn't justify the capital.

**9/ Zero of 7 top-10 RBs since 2010 produced an elite return.**

Not Saquon. Not Zeke. Not even CMC (pick 8, the closest). The best RB outcome still looks mediocre compared to what that pick could have been at QB, EDGE, or OT.

**10/ Rookie Surplus Is the Hidden Majority**

For most players with positive returns, the rookie contract accounts for 50-75% of the total value. Getting starter production at rookie wages for 4 years matters more than the second contract itself.

This is why positions with expensive FA markets (QB, OT, EDGE) dominate.

**11/ Are Teams Drafting Optimally?**

About 30% of first-round picks since 2010 went to positions where the model sees negative returns. Teams systematically overdraft certain positions early and underdraft others late.

The biggest mismatch: too many Round 1 RBs, not enough Round 1 OTs.

[Optimal vs actual allocation chart]

**12/ Key Takeaways**

- QB: Best top-10 investment (if you can scout it)
- OT/EDGE: Peak in late first — trade back if you can
- WR: Round 2 is the sweet spot
- RB: Negative everywhere — the model says wait or sign a FA

But is "never draft RB in Round 1" really a universal law?

That's where things get interesting. Part 2 stress-tests these findings. 👇

## THREAD 2: "What the Sharpe Ratio Can't See"
*Article 2 — The Stress Test*

**1/ What the Sharpe Ratio Can't See**

Last thread, I built a Sharpe ratio for NFL draft picks. The verdict: never draft an RB in Round 1.

I stress-tested that advice. The framework held up — but I found three blind spots that turn a universal rule into a conditional one. 🧵

**2/ Quick Recap**

The Sharpe ratio says: QB is king, OT/EDGE peak late first, WR is a Round 2 play, and RB is negative everywhere.

The advice: if you want a running back, wait until Day 2 or sign a free agent.

But GMs don't draft on average. They draft in specific years, with specific rosters, facing specific prospects.

**3/ Blind Spot #1: Roster Context**

The model assumes every team should always draft the highest-Sharpe position. But what if you already have a franchise QB?

I added a quality gate: a position is only "filled" when the starter plays above threshold, earned a real second contract, and isn't expiring. With that filter, draft efficiency rankings shift dramatically.

**4/ Picks that fill genuine roster needs perform better.**

Need-addressing picks average a higher need-adjusted Sharpe than picks at already-filled positions. A player drafted into opportunity has a clearer path to snaps, production, and a second contract.

The Sharpe ratio tells you which positions have the best *average* returns. But if you already have a franchise QB, that average is irrelevant to you.

**5/ Blind Spot #2: Class Depth**

The Sharpe ratio averages across 13 draft classes. But class quality varies wildly.

Using NFL Next Gen Stats Prospect Scores, I tracked how many Good+ prospects each class produced. Some RB classes have 6-8 quality prospects. Others have 1-2. The model treats them identically.

**6/ The pre-draft signal actually works.**

The correlation between a class's Good+ prospect count and its eventual hit rate is meaningful. Deep classes produce more starters. Thin classes are a crapshoot.

"Never draft RB" is really "never draft RB *in an average class*." In a thin class, the calculus changes.

**7/ The 2026 Class at a Glance**

2026 is loaded at IOL, CB, and WR — deep classes with plenty of Day 2 value.

But RB is the thinnest class in years. Only one Elite-graded prospect: Jeremiyah Love. The gap to RB2 is 17 points — a chasm by NGS standards.

Pass on Love and there's no comparable alternative later.

**8/ Blind Spot #3: Not All Prospects Are Average**

The Sharpe ratio averages over all RBs. But Love's 94.3 Prospect Score ranks 5th all-time among RB prospects since 2010.

Pre-draft elite RBs (top 10% Prospect Score) become starters at dramatically higher rates than the position average. The model's "never draft RB" is really "never draft an *average* RB."

**9/ I re-ran the Sharpe formula with career AV instead of contract value.**

RB jumped from dead last (negative Sharpe) to 6th out of 11 positions with a positive Sharpe. The gap is the market inefficiency: RBs produce more than the market pays them for.

The original Sharpe measures what the *market* values. Career AV measures what the *player* produced.

**10/ The Counterfactual Test**

135 first-round picks since 2010 went to positions where the Sharpe was negative. I tracked the actual next premium player (QB/EDGE/OT/WR) taken in the same draft.

The surplus gap is real and staggering. Even mediocre QB outcomes beat great RB outcomes in surplus terms because the cost-controlled advantage at expensive FA positions is enormous.

**11/ But the Ceiling Tells a Different Story**

Using quantile regression, I projected Love's floor, median, and ceiling outcomes at every draft slot.

His median surplus runs below premium positions at every pick — the Sharpe framework holds.

But his 90th-percentile outcome (a Gurley/CMC-level career) crosses the premium surplus line at a mid-first-round pick. That's the narrow window where the bet makes sense.

**12/ The Timing Problem**

One more thing the surplus math can't see: RBs peak early and decline fast.

RBs produce immediately in year 1, then fade. QBs and WRs keep climbing for years. Teams picking early are usually rebuilding — by the time they're competitive, the RB's best years are gone.

Teams picking later are closer to contention. They can use Love's peak years now.

**13/ Who Should Draft Love?**

9 teams enter 2026 needing an RB. The sweet spot: teams that need a back, pick in the mid-to-late first (where the upside math works), and are close enough to contention to use Love's peak years.

The Sharpe ratio is a great framework. It just can't see the difference between "RB" and *this* RB, in *this* class, for *this* team.

**14/ What Still Holds**

- QB is still king
- Surplus math still favors premium positions early
- Day 2+ RBs are still the default in normal classes
- The Sharpe ratio is one of the best draft strategy tools we have

But "never" is too strong. The right word is "rarely" — and 2026 might be one of those rare years.

**15/ Next up: Part 3.**

The draft will happen. Teams will make their picks. And we'll grade every team on how "Sharpe" they actually were.

Did they follow the model? Did they break the rules for good reasons? Did the Love gamble pay off?

## ARTICLE 3 TEE-UP: "How Sharpe Was Your Draft?"
*Post-Draft Team Grades*

### Concept

After the 2026 NFL Draft, grade every team's picks using the Sharpe framework from Articles 1 and 2. The question isn't "did you draft good players?" (we can't know that yet) — it's "did you make Sharpe-optimal decisions given what we know?"

### Structure

**For each team's draft:**
1. **Sharpe Grade**: Was each pick at a positive-Sharpe position×tier combo? Score each pick against the heatmap.
2. **Need Adjustment**: Did the pick fill a genuine roster hole (Article 2's quality gate)? Partial credit for addressing real needs at low-Sharpe positions.
3. **Class Depth Adjustment**: Did they draft from a deep class (value available later) or a thin class (scarcity premium justified)?
4. **Prospect Separation**: If they took a non-premium position, was the prospect clearly separated from the field? (Love-level gaps vs. interchangeable prospects)

### Key Storylines to Track
- **Who took Love?** Grade the pick against the fair-value pick threshold from Article 2. Was it a team in the sweet spot (need + right pick range + contention window)?
- **Who followed the model?** Teams that went QB/EDGE/OT/WR in Round 1 — did they pick from need or just chase Sharpe?
- **Who broke the rules well?** Teams that drafted non-premium positions but had all three conditions from Article 2 (roster need, thin class, elite prospect).
- **Who broke the rules badly?** Teams that drafted non-premium positions without the justifying conditions.
- **Biggest surplus bets**: Which teams maximized the rookie surplus gap by drafting expensive-FA-market positions?
- **The "what they left on the table" counterfactual**: For each controversial pick, who was the next premium player taken?

### Grading Scale
- **A**: Positive-Sharpe combo + addressed a need + prospect separation (or premium position at right tier)
- **B**: Positive-Sharpe combo but position already filled, OR negative-Sharpe but all three Article 2 conditions met
- **C**: Mixed — some justification, some waste
- **D**: Negative-Sharpe combo without the justifying conditions
- **F**: Premium capital on a negative-Sharpe position when premium alternatives were available and the team had needs there

### Data Needed Post-Draft
- Actual pick-by-pick results (available immediately)
- Map each pick to position group × tier × Sharpe value (from existing data)
- 2026 roster needs (already built in Article 2)
- 2026 class depth by position (already built in Article 2)
- NGS Prospect Scores for all drafted players (available pre-draft)
- The "next premium player taken" for each non-premium pick (same methodology as Article 2's counterfactual)

### Thread Format (Post-Draft)
Quick thread grading the full first round, then a longer article with all rounds.
